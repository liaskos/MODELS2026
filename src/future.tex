\section{Concluding Remarks and Future Plans}\label{sec:conclusions}

We presented an experimental study exploring the suitability of LLMs as substitutes for human participants in evaluating the quality of language vocabularies according to a proposed methodology, Peira. We constructed a data collection instrument intended for humans, reproduced it in the form of an LLM prompt containing the same information, and administered each to a group of humans and a group of LLMs, respectively. We found that the LLM performance level is too distant from that of the specific group of humans to allow performance of the former to be readily considered as a substitute indicator of the performance of the latter. The finding applies to all 17 models that produced usable output, and, generally, the larger the model the larger the deviation.

The result invites follow-up work aimed at fully comprehending why humans perform worse than LLMs even when both groups are given the exact same information and both arguably have the preparation to comprehend that information. Validity threats relevant to this specific study that could be part of the explanation need to first be carefully investigated. A salient concern pertaining to \textit{internal validity} is the differences in the modalities by which information was presented to LLMs and humans. Though LLMs are only given a transcript, humans are given video presentations and visual widgets allowing factors such as the narrator's voice, choice of visuals, and instrument layout to interfere with human performance. Thus, although videos were subtitled and could be rewatched, cases were presented in both video and textual format, and language cheat-sheets with definitions and examples were accessible at all times, the participant may have still found the material confusing and unstimulating due to its presentation format alone -- despite the latter being crafted to support engagement experience and comprehension rather than hinder it. In general, however, comparing human and LLM performance in fair terms can be challenging given the different kinds of measures that need to be applied to each type of agent in order to minimize communication friction and indeed allow for a fair comparison. An additional concern relating to \textit{external validity} is that human participants, despite having self-reported information technology education at the university or college level, may lack the specific interest or background in matters pertinent to iStar-DT. At the same time however, it can be argued that concepts such as \textit{goal}, \textit{effect}, and \textit{wants-to} are readily comprehensible by an even wider population than the one from which our participants were sampled. To these concerns one can add that our investigation was limited to one language and two classification cases. Follow-up studies could clarify the contribution of these factors to the result.

These considerations notwithstanding, the gap between human and LLM performance still invites us to ask what level, if any, of training effort, intensity, detail, and care suffices for motivated humans to reach LLM-level performance. This, in turn, leads us to rethink how processes like Peira are to be utilized. Assuming that the performance of powerful LLMs offers a vocabulary comprehension standard, then Peira, when applied to humans, can be used to measure the \textit{learnability} of the vocabulary or the quality of the training process, rather than the quality of the vocabulary itself. In the iStar-DT case, for example, we could assume that its vocabulary is as well-designed as LLM ratings imply it is, and attribute the discrepancy with human data to low learnability. This would imply that the designers may either call the vocabulary successful but caution that it needs extensive training (surely more than what LLMs require), or re-engineer it to make it more learnable, which may, however, imply expressiveness-suppressing decisions, such as reducing the size of the vocabulary through concept abstraction. Nevertheless, the premise of the above reasoning, i.e., that LLMs offer a standard of comprehension of a language that any future human user can attain if they just receive enough training, needs itself to be tested and qualified. We plan to design and conduct studies that clarify if such reasoning is valid. 

We are also interested in acquiring a more complete picture of how LLMs can assist with the assessment of vocabulary quality. For example, self-reported metrics such as semantic overlaps, and construct deficits and excesses, which Peira elicits from humans, can be investigated when provided by LLMs vis-\`{a}-vis their relevance and validity, through, for example, expert assessment. We also plan to explore whether qualitative feedback and advice from LLMs can be useful in improving the language. 

Finally, in conceptualizing future work on modeling language quality assessment, it is relevant to acknowledge that in a world where LLM agents are also, alongside humans, producers and users of models in practical production environments, LLMs become consumers of quality as well. They are, hence, additional stakeholders to satisfy in designing a language and its training process and material. Developing systematic empirical evaluation practices tailored to LLMs is, thus, another direction for future investigation.
