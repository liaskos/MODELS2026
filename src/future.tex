\section{Concluding Remarks and Future Plans}\label{sec:conclusions}

We reported on an experimental study exploring the suitability of LLMs as substitutes for human participants in evaluating the quality of language vocabularies according to a proposed methodology, Peira. We constructed a data collection instrument intended for humans, reproduced it in the form of an LLM prompt containing the same information, and administered each to a group of humans and a group of LLMs. We found that LLMs' performance level is too distant from that of humans to allow the former to be used as substitutes of the latter, and that performance tends to improve as model size increases in number of parameters. 

The result opens many research questions and opportunities for future investigation. One such pertains to finding explanations for the result, i.e., why humans perform worse than LLMs even when both groups are given the exact same information and both arguably have the preparation to comprehend that material. One shallow answer is the quality of the video training material, which may feature a voice or visuals that the participants found unstimulating or distracting. An additional, perhaps more credible, concern is that the online participants, despite having self-reported information technology education at the university or college level, may lack the specific interest or background on the matters specifically pertinent to iStar-DT. However, one can counter-argue that concepts such as \textit{goal}, \textit{effect}, \textit{wants-to} are readily comprehensible from an even wider population than the one from which our participants were sampled. Regardless, such concerns may be construed, respectively, as \textit{internal} and \textit{external validity} threats of our study and will need follow up investigations to be resolved. 

Such studies that ask what level, if any, of training effort, length, and care suffices for motivated humans to reach LLM-level performance, ask us to rethink how processes like Peira are to be utilized. Assuming that the performance of powerful LLMs offer a vocabulary comprehension standard, Peira, applied then to humans, can be used measure the \textit{learnability} of the vocabulary or the quality of the training process, rather than the quality of the vocabulary itself. In our case, for example, we would assume that iStar-DT's vocabulary is as well-designed as LLM ratings imply it is, and explain the discrepancy with human data to low learnability -- implying that the designers may either call the vocabulary successful but caution that it needs extensive training (surely more than what LLMs require), or re-engineer it to make it more learnable, which may, however, imply expressiveness-suppressing decisions, such as reducing the size of the vocabulary via abstracting concepts. At the same time, the premise of the above reasoning, i.e., that LLMs offer a standard of comprehension of a language that any future human user can attain if they just receive enough training needs itself to be tested and qualified. We plan to design and conduct studies that clarify if such reasoning is valid. 

We are also interested in acquiring a more complete picture of how LLMs can assist with the assessment of vocabulary quality. For example, self-reported metrics such as of semantic overlaps, and of construct deficits and excesses, which Peira elicits from humans, can be investigated when provided by LLMs vis-a-vis their relevance and validity, via, e.g., expert assessment. We plan to explore whether qualitative feedback and advice from LLMs can be useful in improving the language. 

As a final remark, in conceptualizing future work on language quality assessment, it is important to acknowledge that in a world where LLM agents are also, along humans, producers and users of models in practical production environments, LLMs become a consumer of quality as well, meaning that they are an additional stakeholder to satisfy in designing the language and producing its training material. Exploring language design vis-a-vis the role of LLMs in its use is, hence, another direction for future investigation.
